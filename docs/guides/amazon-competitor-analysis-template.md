# Amazon competitor analysis: compare the right products first

{% include article-visual.html %}

A competitor spreadsheet is only useful if the products belong in the same comparison. A premium insulated bottle and a disposable party cup may both appear for “travel drink container,” but their buyers are solving different problems.

Start with the purchase decision—not the biggest list of ASINs. This guide gives you a reusable framework for deciding which competitors to study and what evidence to collect.

## Define the decision before collecting data

Write a one-sentence brief: “We want to understand how comparable products explain their size, intended use and included accessories so we can test a clearer listing.” That produces a more actionable report than “find winning products.”

Record your marketplace, target buyer, product type and price context. Choose a small shortlist you can inspect manually. The number is a working choice, not a statistical rule.

Keep three groups separate:

- **Direct competitors:** a similar product for the same use case.
- **Alternatives:** different products that solve the same buyer problem.
- **Inspiration:** strong presentation worth studying, even when the product is not comparable.

## Use this competitor comparison template

Copy the following structure into your own spreadsheet. Add one row per ASIN; leave unavailable values empty rather than guessing.

| Field | What to record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ASIN and marketplace | Exact product identifier and country | Prevents mixing different listings or markets |
| Comparison group | Direct, alternative or inspiration | Makes the shortlist defensible |
| Product configuration | Size, material, pack count and included items | Explains apparent price differences |
| Observed offer | Price, currency and collection date | Keeps a changing offer in context |
| Buyer promise | Main benefit stated on the listing | Shows what the product asks buyers to believe |
| Keyword evidence | Relevant terms, source and available period | Connects discovery to buyer intent |
| Review evidence | Sample scope, repeated concerns and source comments | Keeps customer interpretation traceable |
| Next test | One proposed change and its validation step | Turns research into a decision |

An illustrative comparison might flag that one listing explains internal dimensions while another only states external dimensions. That suggests testing a clearer size image. It does not prove that either listing converts better.

## Turn the comparison into a short action list

For each idea, ask: Is it relevant to our product? Is the evidence specific enough? Can we test it without making an unsupported claim?

Prioritize one change at a time—for example, clarify pack contents, improve a comparison image or answer a recurring compatibility question. Record what you changed and compare your own outcomes over an appropriate period. Traffic, price, stock and advertising changes can affect the result too.

Avoid copying competitor wording, brand terms or product claims. Their listing is research material, not proof that the same statement applies to your product.

## What this analysis cannot tell you

Public product and keyword data cannot establish a competitor's profitability or your future conversion rate. Provider estimates are not audited seller-account results. A low-star review sample is not a measurement of all buyers.

Use this template to choose better questions and tests, not to turn uncertain data into certainty.
